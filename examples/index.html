WebAssembly (CPU) vs. WebGL (GPU) Skinning Pipelines for the SMPL Avatar Model

Introduction

Parametric body models like SMPL (Skinned Multi-Person Linear model) allow us to represent a massive variety of realistic human body shapes and movements using just a handful of parameters. By adjusting simple controls for height, weight, and joint rotation, we can dynamically generate and animate detailed 3D human figures. This technology is at the core of virtual reality, digital fashion, medical posture analysis, and fitness tracking.

Traditionally, these 3D models required expensive gaming computers and complex software installations. To make virtual avatars accessible to everyone, we must be able to run these models directly inside a standard web browser—rendering smoothly on ordinary laptops, tablets, and mobile phones.

This article compares two different strategies for bringing interactive 3D avatars to the web: executing the math on the device's main processor (CPU) versus offloading the calculations to the graphics card (GPU). Below, you will find interactive pipeline walkthroughs, performance results, and design recommendations for web graphics.

Literature Review

The Mathematical Foundation of SMPL

To build and profile the rendering pipelines, we must first look at the mathematical formulations that define the shape and posture of the SMPL model. SMPL represents a human body as a template mesh T \in \mathbb{R}^{N \times 3} containing N = 6890 vertices, controlled by shape parameters \beta \in \mathbb{R}^{10} (the principal components of body shape variation) and pose parameters \theta \in \mathbb{R}^{72} (representing 24 joints in axis-angle format).

The vertex transformation pipeline proceeds through four main mathematical steps:

1. Shape Blend Shapes

The shape parameters \beta scale predefined directions of body variation (e.g., height, weight, proportions) to deform the template mesh:

T_s(\beta) = T_{template} + B_S(\beta)

where B_S(\beta) = \sum_{k=1}^{10} \beta_k S_k is the shape deformation vector, and S_k \in \mathbb{R}^{6890 \times 3} represent the shape principal component displacements. On the CPU, this is mapped as flat float arrays of shape (6890, 3, 10), accessed with strided strides of v \times 30 + k \times 3 + \text{axis}.

2. Pose Blend Shapes

To avoid artifacts like the "collapsing elbow" during joint rotations, SMPL adds pose-dependent dynamic offsets before skinning:

T_p(\theta, \beta) = T_s(\beta) + B_P(\theta)

where B_P(\theta) = \sum_{n=1}^{207} (R_n(\theta) - R_n(\theta^*)) P_n is the pose-corrective offset vector, R_n(\theta) represents the local rotation matrix of joint n, and P_n \in \mathbb{R}^{6890 \times 3} represents the pose-corrective displacements. The pose feature vector is computed by taking the local rotation matrices of the 23 active joints (excluding the global root orientation) in SO(3) and subtracting the identity matrix I (yielding a 207-dimensional vector: 23 \text{ joints} \times 9 \text{ matrix elements}).

3. Joint Regression

The 3D locations of the 24 joints J(\beta) are regressed from the shaped mesh template:

J(\beta) = \mathcal{J} T_s(\beta)

where \mathcal{J} \in \mathbb{R}^{24 \times 6890} is a sparse regressor matrix learned from data. For performance, rather than doing dense matrix multiplication, we perform sparse accumulation by looping only over non-zero weights in the pre-computed sparse index tables.

4. Linear Blend Skinning (LBS)

Finally, the posed vertices are mapped to their world coordinates using joint transformations recursively computed along the kinematic tree:

v_i' = \sum_{j=1}^{24} w_{i, j} G_j(\theta, J(\beta)) G_{j, \text{rest}}^{-1} v_{i, \text{posed}}

where w_{i, j} is the skinning weight defining the influence of joint j on vertex i, G_j(\theta, J(\beta)) \in \mathbb{R}^{4 \times 4} is the global transformation matrix of joint j computed by Forward Kinematics (FK), and G_{j, \text{rest}}^{-1} is the inverse rest pose transform which moves the rest-pose vertex back to the bone's local space before applying the posed transformation.

Linear Blend Skinning Constraints
Each vertex in the SMPL model is typically influenced by multiple bones. To perform LBS efficiently, we restrict the influence to the top 4 most dominant joints per vertex and normalize their weights to sum to 1.0. This matches the native constraints of GPU hardware skinning engines.

Related Work

Evaluating parametric human body models in real-time has been a major research focus across computer vision, computer graphics, and web systems engineering. Existing systems can be broadly categorized into statistical model optimization, desktop performance-oriented engines, and web-based character skinning runtimes.

Statistical Body Models & Reference Software

The Skinned Multi-Person Linear (SMPL) model (Loper et al. [2015]) and its expressive extension SMPL-X (Pavlakos et al. [2019]) established the mathematical standard for representing parameterized humanoid meshes. However, their canonical implementations are written in Python and rely on heavy frameworks like PyTorch or NumPy. While suitable for training and offline mesh-fitting pipelines, these runtimes introduce substantial latency and are restricted to native local interpreter environments, which prevents their deployment in browser sandboxes.

More recent advances in parametric body modeling expand the demographic range and structural granularity of statistical human representations:

  • All-Age Parametric Modeling (ANNY): The ANNY model () addresses a major limitation of standard SMPL (which primarily focuses on adult body proportions) by introducing an open-source, fully-differentiable 3D parametric human model designed for all ages—ranging from infants to the elderly. ANNY builds upon parametric mesh topologies and WHO anthropometric statistics to provide continuous phenotype control over age, height, and weight. While ANNY provides crucial expressive capabilities for pediatric and geriatric modeling, its evaluation still relies on Python/PyTorch pipelines. Scope Note: Optimizing multi-age parametric models like ANNY is outside the immediate scope of this paper; our current work focuses strictly on performance-optimizing the canonical, adult-focused SMPL model for real-time client-side web rendering.
  • High-Fidelity Anthropometric Ecosystems (GNM): Google's Generative aNthropometric Model (GNM) () introduces an open-source ecosystem of scan-based parametric human models. In contrast to SMPL's whole-body linear blendshape formulation (N = 6,890 vertices, 10 shape components, 24 joints), models like GNM Head focus on high-granularity parametric facial identity (253 parameters) and expression (383 parameters) calibrated directly from 3D scans. While GNM releases target desktop DCC tools (Blender, Maya, Houdini) via PyTorch and JAX, its underlying parametric shape principles share similarities with statistical body representations like SMPL.

Performance-Oriented Desktop runtimes

To support real-time desktop applications, several native C++ implementations have been developed. Libraries like SMPLpp and smplxpp reconstruct the model's forward pass in C++, utilizing libTorch (the PyTorch C++ library) to enable CUDA-accelerated evaluation on workstations. More recently, torchure_smplx optimized this pipeline to achieve sub-millisecond execution for batch processing. Although these desktop engines deliver high performance, their heavy dependency on CUDA drivers, local compiled binaries, and massive runtime footprints makes them incompatible with client-side web sandboxes.

Web-Based Character Skinning & WebGL Limits

Web-based rendering frameworks like Three.js, Babylon.js, and specialized humanoid formats like three-vrm provide built-in support for Linear Blend Skinning (LBS). In standard configurations, bone matrices are computed in JavaScript on the CPU and uploaded as uniform arrays to the vertex shader. However, standard uniform-based skinning fails to support parametric body models:

  • Lack of Blendshape Offsets: Standard engines lack native support for shape and pose dynamic offsets before skeletal skinning.
  • Uniform Vector Limitations: WebGL enforces a strict hardware limit on uniform storage (MAX_VERTEX_UNIFORM_VECTORS, typically 256 or 1024 float vectors). The dense displacement tables for the 6,890 SMPL vertices contain thousands of components, making uniform storage impossible.

Our texture-packed WebGL GPU shader architecture bypasses these limitations. By packing the dense displacement tables into 2D floating-point textures and performing parallel lookups via texelFetch in the vertex shader, we achieve full compatibility with the SMPL parameter space while avoiding both WebGL uniform vector limits and JS-WASM boundary copy latency.

Systems Architecture Overview

Deploying parametric human avatars in real-time traditionally requires a heavy Python-based machine learning stack consisting of PyTorch, JAX, and native CUDA dependencies. To bypass this friction for web browsers, the workstation-grade C++ runtime h3d.cpp consolidation enables native model evaluation. This study analyzes the performance and systems trade-offs of the two client-side architectures implemented in its web frontend (smpl-web):

  • Pipeline A (WebAssembly CPU): Compiles the C++ SMPL implementation to browser-executable bytecode using Emscripten with SIMD vectorization (-O3 -msimd128). All model computations—including Rodrigues rotations, shape/pose blendshapes, forward kinematics, and skinning—are executed sequentially in WebAssembly on the CPU.
  • Pipeline B (WebGL GPU Shader): Offloads the model's parallel math to the GPU via custom WebGL vertex shaders, bypassing WebAssembly. Displacement tables are packed into 2D floating-point textures during initialization, allowing the vertex shader to perform parallel lookups via texelFetch, while the JavaScript thread only handles skeletal updates.

The subsequent sections analyze the critical bottlenecks of these approaches, including WebAssembly-WebGL heap boundary copy latency, vertex uniform limits, and parallel GPU texturing costs.

Two Rendering Philosophies on the Web

To evaluate parametric human body models in a web browser, h3d.cpp implements two distinct rendering philosophies. It is critical to note that WebGL uses the device's physical GPU directly through a secure browser interface that translates JavaScript API calls into native graphics commands (OpenGL ES, Metal, Vulkan, or Direct3D). Furthermore, WebGL shaders are not limited to rendering-only math; they execute arbitrary floating-point numerical computations inside the graphics pipeline.

Pipeline A: WASM CPU Engine

Compiles the C++ SMPL implementation directly to WebAssembly using Emscripten with SIMD vectorization (-O3 -msimd128).

CPU (WASM) Execution:

  • Shape blend (B_S) & Pose blend (B_P)
  • Rodrigues rotations & Joint regression
  • Forward Kinematics (FK) & Linear Blend Skinning
  • Normal Estimation & Vertex generation

GPU Role:

Acts strictly as a display device. Receives 6,890 fully-computed 3D vertex positions and normals uploaded across the CPU-GPU bus every frame and rasterizes them.

Pipeline B: WebGL (GPU) Shader Engine

Offloads the model's parallel mathematics to the GPU via custom WebGL vertex shaders, bypassing WebAssembly.

CPU (JavaScript) Execution:

  • Joint regression (cached/sparse)
  • Pose feature vector computation (207 dims)
  • Uploads small control uniforms (\beta, pose features, bone matrices)

GPU (Device Shader Cores) Execution:

Executes displacement lookups via texelFetch, shape/pose correctives, sparse LBS, normal estimation, AND immediate rendering in parallel across thousands of shader cores.

Performance Analysis

To evaluate the execution efficiency of the WASM CPU and WebGL(GPU) backends, the web viewer runs a standardized benchmark suite directly in the browser. Measuring raw frame time (the total duration of the render loop tick) is handled via performance.now(). However, because WebGL drawing commands are executed asynchronously by the GPU, CPU-side timers cannot measure hardware execution directly.

To obtain precise measurements of actual GPU hardware execution time, the benchmark suite utilizes the WebGL Disjoint Timer Query Extension (EXT_disjoint_timer_query_webgl2 or its WebGL 1 fallback). This allows the application to query the GPU for the precise elapsed time spent processing drawing calls, independent of browser garbage collection, page layout reflows, or scheduling delays.

Asynchronous GPU Profiling Workflow

Because WebGL drawing commands are sent to the GPU queue asynchronously, measuring their duration requires a deferred query loop:

  1. Begin Query: A query object is created and begun using gl.beginQuery(extTimerQuery.TIME_ELAPSED_EXT, query) immediately before drawing.
  2. End Query: The query is stopped with gl.endQuery() right after the Three.js renderer.render() call.
  3. Enqueue: The query object is placed into a query queue.
  4. Poll and Resolve: On subsequent frames, the application polls the query status via gl.getQueryParameter(query, gl.QUERY_RESULT_AVAILABLE). Once available, the GPU time is fetched via gl.getQueryParameter(query, gl.QUERY_RESULT) and converted from nanoseconds to milliseconds, while the query object is deleted.

By combining WebGL disjoint queries with internal C++ high-resolution timers exposed through WASM bindings (e.g., getShapeTime(), getPoseTime(), getSkinningTime()), we receive a complete breakdown of the execution costs across every stage of the animation pipeline.

Mathematical FLOPs Profiling

To understand the computational complexity and performance profiles of both pipelines, we analyzed the source code of both the C++ reference engine in h3d.cpp and the WebGL client in smpl-web/src/main.js. For a single character with N = 6,890 vertices, F = 13,776 faces, and |\beta| = 10 shape components, we profiled the exact number of Floating-Point Operations (FLOPs) required by the WASM CPU Pipeline and the WebGL GPU Pipeline.

1. Rodrigues / Pose Feature 2,136 ~2,205 0
2. Shape Blendshapes (B_S) 413,400 0 413,400
3. Joint Regression (J) 992,160 ~4,320 * 0
4. Pose Correctives (B_P) 8,578,119 0 8,557,380
5. Forward Kinematics (FK) 5,333 5,333 0
6. Linear Blend Skinning (LBS) 5,484,440 0 1,012,830
7. Normal Estimation & Skinning 399,524 0 661,440
Grand Total (Per Frame) 15,875,112 ~11,858** 10,645,050

* Joint regression on the CPU in Pipeline B is optimized with a sparse CSR structure in JavaScript, executing only on non-zero weights.
** During regular playback frames, joint regression is cached and only FK and Rodrigues run, reducing CPU workload to 7,538 FLOPs.

This profiling reveals key architectural insights:

  • The CPU Offloading Factor: In Pipeline A, the CPU is responsible for the entire 15.88 million FLOPs of mathematical calculations per frame. In Pipeline B, the CPU's responsibilities are reduced to only ~11,858 FLOPs (a 99.93% reduction in CPU floating-point operations).
  • Sparse vs. Dense Operations: The C++ reference CPU implementation computes LBS skinning as a dense matrix combination across all 24 joints for every vertex (5.48 million FLOPs). Conversely, the WebGL pipeline leverages GPU hardware skinning which is sparse—restricting bone influences to a maximum of 4 joints per vertex, reducing LBS operations to 1.01 million FLOPs on the GPU.
  • The Texture Fetch Cost: On the GPU, the 10.65 million FLOPs of blendshapes and correctives are executed in parallel across the GPU cores. However, this is accompanied by 1,495,130 texture fetches per frame to query the displacement tables. Because WebGL texture fetches are latency-tolerant on modern GPU architectures, this offloading is highly efficient and leaves the CPU completely free to maintain UI interactivity and high frame rates.

Architectural Drivers of the WebGL GPU Speedup

Both rendering pipelines utilize the exact same physical GPU hardware for final display rasterization. The massive performance advantage of Pipeline B (CPU computation dropping from 4.35 ms to 0.16 ms) stems from four fundamental architectural distinctions:

  1. Massive Parallel Execution: In the WASM CPU engine, a single thread (or a small set of SIMD vector units) processes the N = 6,890 vertices sequentially. In WebGL GPU mode, thousands of independent shader cores execute the vertex shader simultaneously—processing many vertices in parallel across GPU execution units.
  2. Drastic CPU-to-GPU Data Transfer Reduction: With WASM, every frame requires uploading 6,890 vertex position vectors and 6,890 normal vectors across the CPU-GPU bus (a substantial data transfer). With WebGL, the CPU uploads only 10 shape parameters (\beta), 207 pose features, and 24 bone matrices (a tiny uniform payload of a few kilobytes). The deformed mesh vertices never cross the bus—they are generated directly inside GPU memory.
  3. GPU Memory Locality: Displacement tables are stored as floating-point 2D textures in GPU VRAM during initialization. Every frame, the GPU queries textures locally via texelFetch, computes offsets, skins the mesh, and renders without requesting data from host RAM.
  4. Compute-and-Render Pipeline: WASM forces a serial workflow: CPU computes mesh → bus transfer → GPU draws. WebGL fuses computation and rendering: GPU computes mesh in vertex shader → immediate GPU rasterization.
Analogy: The Factory Model
WASM CPU Pipeline: A single engineer (the CPU) manually constructs all 6,890 parts sequentially, then ships the finished parts to a factory (the GPU) purely for painting. The factory workers remain idle during manufacturing.

WebGL GPU Pipeline: The engineer sends only a concise blueprint (small control uniforms). Thousands of workers inside the factory (GPU shader cores) build their assigned parts simultaneously and paint them immediately on site.

Conclusion

The h3d.cpp project's exploration of web rendering techniques demonstrates that there is no one-size-fits-all solution for real-time character animation. WebAssembly provides a robust, zero-maintenance mechanism to share complex model math directly with high-performance desktop implementations, but faces scaling limits due to browser memory architectures and CPU-GPU bandwidth. Conversely, the WebGL(GPU) backend offers fluid performance for crowds and avatars, but requires careful CPU-GPU bandwidth management when active pose correctives require uploading vertex offsets every frame.
